# Fast path: what's different in C++

- **The libraries.** **glm** is the C++ header-only library that mirrors GLSL (`glm::vec3`, `glm::mat4`, `glm::lookAt`, `glm::perspective`, column-major, right-handed by default); **Eigen** is the general linear-algebra workhorse (`Eigen::Matrix4d`, `Eigen::Affine3d`, `Quaterniond`) with expression templates and SIMD; **DirectXMath** (SSE/NEON, row-vector convention); **Blaze**, **Boost.QVM**. Rust has `glam`/`nalgebra`, Python `numpy`. Nobody writes this for production, but everybody has to know what these conventions mean when reading their docs.
- **SIMD.** A `Vec3` of doubles is 24 bytes; engines use `float` and pad to 16 bytes (`alignas(16)`, a fourth lane) so one SSE instruction does a whole add or dot. `std::experimental::simd` and C++26 `std::simd` standardise the intrinsics. For a 4x4 float multiply, SSE needs 16 multiplies and 12 adds worth of instructions instead of 64 + 48 scalar ones.
- **`float` vs `double`.** GPUs and games use `float` (7 digits): the `1e-9` rotation errors in these tests become `1e-6`, large worlds need camera-relative rendering, and depth buffers need reversed-Z to survive far planes. The algorithms are identical; the edge cases (overflow thresholds around `1e38`, denormals around `1e-38`) move.
- **`std::hypot` and `std::numeric` helpers.** C++17 added `std::hypot(x, y, z)`; it is the standard answer to `length`, implemented with scaling and sometimes extra-precision tricks; `std::lerp` (C++20) is the standard blend (`a + t * (b - a)` with exactness at `t = 0, 1`), which for vectors is the `a * (1 - t) + b * t` you wrote when the endpoints are huge.
- **Operators and copies.** Small value types with overloaded operators are free: the compiler passes a `Vec3` in registers and inlines everything (that is why the `clean` tier bans `virtual` and heap containers: a `std::vector<double>` in a matrix would turn every multiply into an allocation). Expression templates (Eigen, Blaze) go further and fuse `a + b * c` into one loop with no temporaries.
- **Inverse of rigid transforms.** For `M = T * R` (rotation then translation) the inverse is `R^T` followed by `-R^T t`: 9 multiplications instead of the 100+ of a general inverse, and no failure case. Normal vectors transform by the **inverse transpose** of the model matrix, not by the matrix itself: under non-uniform scaling the two differ, which is the classic "my lighting is wrong on stretched objects" bug.
