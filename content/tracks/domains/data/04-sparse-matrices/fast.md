# Fast path: what's different in C++

- **The libraries.** In practice you use **Eigen** (`Eigen::SparseMatrix<double>` is CSC or CSR, with `setFromTriplets` doing exactly your `from_triplets`), **SuiteSparse**, Intel **MKL**, **cuSPARSE** on GPUs, **Boost.uBLAS** or **Armadillo** (`sp_mat`). SciPy's `csr_matrix` is the same three arrays. The 2020s standard-library story is `std::mdspan` for dense and nothing for sparse.
- **Formats.** CSR is good at row access and `A x`; **CSC** (columns) is good at `A^T x` and factorisations; **COO** (triplets) is for building; **ELL** and **SELL-C-sigma** pad rows to equal length for SIMD and GPU use; **BSR** stores small dense blocks. Transposing a CSR gives you the CSC of the original for free: the three arrays are the same.
- **SpMV is memory-bound.** The arithmetic is one multiply-add per stored value, but every value drags an index and a scattered read of `x[col]` with it. That is why SpMV runs at a few percent of a CPU's peak FLOPS and why reordering rows/columns for locality (reverse Cuthill-McKee) matters more than micro-optimising the loop.
- **Index types.** `std::size_t` indices cost 8 bytes each; production code uses `std::uint32_t` (up to 4 billion) or templated index types to halve the traffic. The dimension cap you wrote is the same idea made explicit.
- **Parallel SpMV** is easy for CSR (rows are independent: `#pragma omp parallel for`, or `std::for_each(std::execution::par, ...)`); the transposed product is a *scatter* and needs atomics or per-thread buffers, which is a good reason to materialise `A^T` once when you need it often.
- **Stable sorting vs unspecified order.** `std::sort` is allowed to reorder equal keys, so duplicates would be summed in an arbitrary order and results would vary between standard libraries. `std::stable_sort` (or sorting by `(row, col, original index)`) makes the output deterministic.
