# Fast path: what's different in C++

- **`std::mdspan` (C++23).** The standard library now has what you just wrote: `std::mdspan<T, std::dextents<size_t, 2>, std::layout_stride>` is a non-owning multi-dimensional view with arbitrary strides, `std::submdspan` (C++26) is `block()`, and `layout_left`/`layout_right` are column-major/row-major. Before it, everyone wrote their own, as here, or used Eigen's `Map<..., Stride<>>`, Boost.uBLAS's `matrix_range`, or `xtensor`.
- **Views are the C++ way to avoid copies** (`std::span`, `std::string_view`, `std::ranges::views`). The rule that comes with them: a view must not outlive what it points at. Returning `View` from a function that built a local `Matrix` is a dangling pointer.
- **Column-major vs row-major.** C and C++ arrays are row-major; Fortran, MATLAB, BLAS/LAPACK and Eigen's default are column-major. Passing a matrix to BLAS often means *transposing it for free*: a row-major `A` seen by column-major code is `A^T`. Strides are what make that a no-op.
- **Real GEMM.** Production libraries add a second level of blocking (registers), pack the tiles into contiguous scratch memory, and use SIMD intrinsics; OpenBLAS and MKL reach 90 percent of the CPU's peak. The three-level loop here is the idea in its simplest form. Compilers can also tile loops automatically (`-floop-block`, Polly) but rarely beat a hand-written kernel.
- **`std::vector<T>` of `T = double`** is contiguous and 16-byte aligned from `operator new`; for AVX you often want 32 or 64 bytes (`std::aligned_alloc`, `std::pmr` with an aligned arena, or `alignas`).
- **Numerical accuracy.** Matrix products of floating-point numbers accumulate rounding error ~`k * eps`. Blocking changes nothing here as long as the order is preserved; changing the order (e.g. pairwise or SIMD-lane sums) is faster and usually *more* accurate, but gives different bits. Decide on purpose.
